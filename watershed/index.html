How Watershed works

Every raindrop is simulated. A drop lands somewhere, rolls downhill, and speeds up on steep ground. Fast water can carry more soil, so it scrapes some off the slope. When it slows down, on flat ground or when climbing out of a hollow, it drops part of its load. Drops also evaporate as they go. Thousands of drops a second add up to gullies, valleys, alluvial fans and, where they reach the sea, deltas.

Rivers and lakes are worked out separately: every hollow is filled with water up to the height where it would spill over, and each cell passes its water to the lowest neighbour. A cell that drains a large upstream area is drawn as a river.

Scree lets slopes steeper than about 48° slump. Uplift pushes the high ground up again, so the land can reach a balance between mountain building and erosion instead of wearing flat.

The droplet model follows Hans Theobald Beyer's 2015 thesis on particle-based hydraulic erosion. Lakes and drainage use the priority-flood algorithm. All of it runs in your browser.
